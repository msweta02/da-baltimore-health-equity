# Where Baltimore Communities Most Need Navigation, Advocacy, Literacy and Caregiver Support

**Source:** BNIA Vital Signs, 2010–2023 · 55 Community Statistical Areas
**Current conditions:** 2023 · **Health outcomes:** 2018 (see *Limitations and data-quality concerns*)

---

## The short answer

**Start with Southwest Baltimore, Greenmount East, Clifton-Berea and Midway/Coldstream, plus
Upton/Druid Heights.** These five hold 44,030 residents. Each sits in the highest need tier
*and* has been among the city's ten worst for life expectancy in seven or eight of the eight
years measured — chronic failure, not a single bad year.

| Community | Need score | Residents | Life expectancy 2018 | vs. city | Worst-10 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Southwest Baltimore** | 81.6 | 13,189 | 66.4 | −6.3 yrs | 8 of 8 |
| **Greenmount East** | 85.0 | 6,955 | 67.7 | −5.0 yrs | 8 of 8 |
| **Clifton-Berea** | 78.8 | 7,577 | 67.4 | −5.3 yrs | 8 of 8 |
| **Midway/Coldstream** | 76.6 | 7,393 | 68.1 | −4.6 yrs | 8 of 8 |
| **Upton/Druid Heights** | 77.9 | 8,916 | 68.5 | −4.2 yrs | 7 of 8 |

**Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park** scores second-highest in the city on need (84.3) and is a
strong candidate, but it appears among the ten worst for life expectancy in only **three of
eight years** — acute need, less entrenched. Our robustness testing found it holds its rank
better than three of the five above, so we carry it as a **sixth candidate**; see *How far do
these findings hold up?*

Across all 55 communities: **13 highest need, 15 high, 9 moderate, 18 lower.** Together the
highest tier holds 129,237 residents, **22% of Baltimore.**

Note that these five are the answer to *where to begin*. They are not the answer to *who
needs each service* — those geographies differ sharply, and are set out in the next section.

## The finding that should shape targeting

**There is no single "neediest" community, because these four services are needed in
different places.**

Each service line was scored separately. The rankings only partly agree — caregiver-support
need overlaps with health-literacy need by a rank correlation of just **0.39**. A single
combined ranking would send every service to the same neighborhoods and miss at least one
population entirely.

Where each service is most needed:

**Health literacy**

| Community | Score | Hispanic residents |
|---|---|---|
| Westport/Mount Winans/Lakeland | 100 | 40% |
| Southeastern | 98 | 45% |
| Brooklyn/Curtis Bay/Hawkins Point | 96 | 31% |
| Orangeville/East Highlandtown | 95 | 44% |

**None of these appear in the overall top ten.** Their Hispanic population runs 31–45%
against a citywide median of 6%. This is a language-access need, not a deprivation need —
the underlying measure is adults without a U.S. high school diploma, which in an immigrant
population substantially reflects schooling completed abroad rather than limited capability.
Services here should mean Spanish-language materials and interpretation. Their life
expectancy is at or near the city median, so these are people who struggle to *use* the
system rather than people the system is already failing.

**Navigation support** — Oldtown/Middle East (90), Greenmount East (88), Midway/Coldstream
(88), Pimlico/Arlington/Hilltop (87), Cherry Hill (87). In Sandtown-Winchester, **58.5% of
households have no vehicle** against a citywide median of 27%. Reaching the appointment is
the binding constraint.

**Caregiver support** — Pimlico/Arlington/Hilltop (82), Southern Park Heights (82), Greater
Mondawmin (82), Clifton-Berea (80), Greenmount East (78). This is the most distinctive
geography of the four and the one most likely to be mis-targeted if a single index is used.

**Patient advocacy / complex care** — Upton/Druid Heights (100), Penn North/Reservoir Hill
(97), Downtown/Seton Hill (93), Greenmount East (92), Sandtown-Winchester (90).

## Recommended starting point

Need alone is the wrong basis for a first placement. A community can score high in one bad
year, and a very needy community with few residents reaches fewer people per dollar spent than
a slightly less needy one with many. We therefore weighed three things together: **how acute
the need is**, **how long it has persisted**, and **how many people it reaches.**

Persistence was measured by counting how many of the eight years from 2011 to 2018 each
community ranked among the ten worst in the city for life expectancy. A community that has
been failing for eight straight years is a different proposition from one that dipped once.

### The four communities that meet every test

These rank in the highest need tier **and** were among the ten worst for life expectancy in
**all eight years measured** — chronic, not episodic:

| Community | Need | Residents | Life expectancy | vs. city | Worst-10 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Southwest Baltimore** | 81.6 | 13,189 | 66.4 | −6.3 | 8 of 8 |
| **Greenmount East** | 85.0 | 6,955 | 67.7 | −5.0 | 8 of 8 |
| **Clifton-Berea** | 78.8 | 7,577 | 67.4 | −5.3 | 8 of 8 |
| **Midway/Coldstream** | 76.6 | 7,393 | 68.1 | −4.6 | 8 of 8 |

Add **Upton/Druid Heights** (need 77.9, 8,916 residents, worst-10 in seven of eight years),
which registers the **highest complex-care need in the city** — the 100th percentile for
overdose and narcotics calls.

Together these five cover **44,030 residents**.

### If only one placement is possible

**Southwest Baltimore.** It combines the largest population of any chronically failing
community (13,189), the **worst life-expectancy gap in the city at 6.3 years below
citywide**, need in the highest tier, and eight-of-eight persistence. No other community
matches it on all four.

### What the evidence does *not* support

**Greenmount East scores highest on need (85.0) but is the smallest of the group** at 6,955
residents. If reach matters, it should not automatically be first despite topping the
ranking — this is exactly the trade-off a need score alone conceals.

Conversely, **Greater Rosemont (15,099 residents) and Brooklyn/Curtis Bay (13,479)** are the
largest highest-tier communities but appear in the worst-10 for life expectancy only once
each. Their need is real but recent or milder; they are candidates for a second wave, not
the first.

**Morrell Park/Violetville** sits in the highest tier yet has never appeared in the worst ten
and is only 0.3 years below citywide life expectancy. Its score is driven by literacy and
navigation barriers without the corresponding health burden — worth noting before it is
treated as equivalent to the others.

### Scale of the opportunity

**129,237 residents — 22% of Baltimore — live in the 13 highest-need communities.** The
remaining tiers hold 21% (high), 17% (moderate) and 40% (lower). Concentrating on the
highest tier is therefore not a narrow intervention; it addresses roughly one resident in
five.

### Two practical notes

**Language capacity changes the list.** If Spanish-language services are available, Westport,
Southeastern, Brooklyn/Curtis Bay and Orangeville become strong candidates on health literacy
alone. Without that capacity, a placement there will not reach the people who need it.

**A single East Baltimore hub could serve four communities — but not the five recommended.**
Checked against the Community Statistical Area boundary map, the five recommended communities
are mostly *not* neighbours: only Clifton-Berea and Midway/Coldstream share a border.
Southwest Baltimore, Greenmount East and Upton/Druid Heights each stand alone.

There is, however, a genuine contiguous corridor in East Baltimore:

> **Greenmount East — Oldtown/Middle East — Clifton-Berea — Midway/Coldstream**

These four form an unbroken chain of about **32,000 residents**, with Oldtown/Middle East as
the connective link between Greenmount East and the other two. A hub sited in or near Oldtown
could plausibly serve all four. Note that this argument brings Oldtown/Middle East into scope
on geography, even though it did not qualify on persistence.

Southwest Baltimore — our single strongest candidate — is geographically separate and would
need its own placement.

## What the conditions look like on the ground

The four dimensions were built from these measures, all 2023:

| Dimension | Measured by |
|---|---|
| Health literacy | Adults 25+ without a high school diploma |
| Navigation barriers | Households with no vehicle; households with no internet |
| Caregiver load | Residents 65+; female-headed households with children |
| Complex care need | Overdose calls and narcotics calls per 1,000 residents |

Each community was ranked against the other 54 on every measure, converted to a 0–100
percentile, then averaged within each dimension. The four dimensions are weighted equally in
the overall score.

## What relates to health outcomes

We tested every available social and economic condition against life expectancy across the
55 communities, using 2018 — the most recent year holding both health and socioeconomic
data. Grouping them by domain shows where the relationships concentrate:

| Domain | Typical strength | Strongest single measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | 0.71 | Households earning over $75,000 (+0.81) |
| **Safety** | 0.55 | Shootings per 1,000 residents (−0.77) |
| **Housing** | 0.48 | Properties with housing violations (−0.80) |
| **Family structure** | 0.39 | Female-headed households with children (−0.83) |
| Civic participation | 0.34 | — |
| Transport access | 0.32 | Public transport to work (−0.71) |
| Race composition | 0.25 | White residents (+0.74) |
| Employment | 0.23 | Unemployment rate (−0.68) |
| Environment | 0.23 | — |
| Local business | 0.09 | — |

*Typical strength is the median absolute correlation across the measures in that domain;
0.7 and above is a strong relationship at this scale, below 0.2 is essentially none.*

### Income level, not employment status

Income is the strongest domain by a clear margin — but **employment is one of the weakest.**
Median strength is 0.71 for income against 0.23 for employment. Unemployment does track life
expectancy (−0.68), yet most employment measures barely move with it.

The implication is uncomfortable but useful: in Baltimore, *having* a job does not
distinguish a healthy community from an unhealthy one nearly as well as *what the job pays*.
Programmes aimed at employment alone would be targeting a weaker signal than programmes
aimed at income adequacy.

### Housing condition, not housing tenure

Housing is a strong domain, and the specific measure matters. Properties with housing
violations correlate at −0.80 and vacant/abandoned properties nearly as strongly, while
ownership rates are weaker. **The physical condition of housing tracks health more closely
than who owns it.** Housing violations and vacancy are also the most stable predictors over
time, holding their strength across the whole 2011–2023 period.

One qualification, set out in full later: housing condition is a powerful marker of *which*
communities are unhealthy, but when we test whether **changes** in housing condition track
**changes** in life expectancy, the relationship nearly vanishes. It identifies where the
problem is; it is weaker evidence that improving it would move outcomes.

### Age structure is not a factor

This one is worth stating plainly because it is often assumed. The share of residents aged
65 and over has **almost no relationship with life expectancy** (+0.20), and neither does the
share under 5 (−0.07). Communities are not unhealthy because they are old.

This makes sense on reflection — life expectancy is already adjusted for age structure by
construction — but it means age should be treated as a *service-planning* variable (older
populations need caregiver support) rather than as a driver of poor outcomes.

### Local business activity shows nothing

Neighbourhood business density, bank branches and job counts sit at 0.09 — no meaningful
relationship with life expectancy at all. Economic activity located *in* a community does not
appear to translate into health for the people living there, at least at this geography.

### The same conditions surface across different outcomes

The pattern is not an artefact of one measure. Low educational attainment is the strongest
predictor of both **low birth weight** and **teen pregnancy**. Housing violations and vacancy
are the strongest predictors of **narcotics-related service calls** and remain so in 2023.
Race composition is the strongest single correlate of **infant mortality** (−0.61 with the
share of white residents), which is a marker of structural inequity rather than an
explanation in itself.

### What the same analysis shows in 2023

Everything above is 2018, because that is the last year holding a health outcome. It is fair
to ask whether those relationships still describe Baltimore today. Two health-adjacent
measures — overdose and narcotics service calls — run to 2023 and let us check.

**Overdose calls per 1,000 residents, strongest correlates in 2023:**

| Correlation | Condition |
|---|---|
| +0.84 | Common assault calls per 1,000 |
| **−0.80** | **Owner-occupied housing** |
| +0.76 | Shootings per 1,000 |
| +0.75 | Vacant and abandoned properties |
| +0.73 | Properties with housing violations |
| +0.69 | Households with no vehicle |
| +0.68 | Children living below the poverty line |

**Narcotics calls per 1,000 residents, 2023:** vacant and abandoned properties (+0.75),
housing violations (+0.70), demolition permits (+0.67), owner-occupied housing (−0.67),
unemployment (+0.66).

The picture holds. Housing condition and ownership, poverty, and violence remain the
conditions that travel with poor health-related outcomes — the same families of measures that
predicted life expectancy in 2018.

**What has shifted since 2018** is instructive:

| Relationship | 2018 | 2023 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overdose calls vs housing violations | +0.53 | +0.73 | **+0.20** |
| Narcotics calls vs unemployment | +0.44 | +0.66 | **+0.21** |
| Overdose calls vs vacant properties | +0.62 | +0.75 | +0.13 |
| Overdose calls vs owner-occupancy | −0.70 | −0.80 | −0.10 |
| Narcotics calls vs vacant properties | +0.82 | +0.75 | −0.07 |

Housing condition and unemployment have become **more** tightly bound to substance-related
service demand, not less. Whatever changed in Baltimore between 2018 and 2023, it did not
loosen the link between deteriorating housing and health crisis — it tightened it.

One caveat carries throughout: these are **service-demand measures**, recording 911 activity.
They reflect policing patterns as well as underlying need, and should not be read as health
status. They are used here because no actual health outcome exists after 2019.

### Can we say which factors *cause* shorter lives?

Not from correlations, and the reason is worth understanding rather than treating as
boilerplate. We tested it directly.

Taking one representative measure from each domain — income, housing condition, violence,
family structure and civic participation — and modelling life expectancy across the 55
communities:

| Domain | Explains alone | Explains *uniquely* |
|---|---|---|
| Family structure | 68.3% | 1.7% |
| Civic participation | 64.2% | 2.0% |
| Housing condition | 63.6% | 1.9% |
| Income | 61.4% | **0.0%** |
| Violence | 59.3% | 2.8% |
| **All five together** | **83.9%** | |

Each factor on its own explains around two thirds of why life expectancy differs between
communities. Yet **no factor explains more than 2.8% that the others do not already
explain** — and income explains nothing at all once the rest are known. Their individual
contributions sum to 317% of a total that is only 84%, because they are overwhelmingly
measuring the same thing.

Splitting the shared explanation fairly between them gives an almost perfectly even result:

| Share of the 84% explained | Domain |
|---|---|
| 18.2 pts | Family structure |
| 17.1 pts | Housing condition |
| 17.1 pts | Civic participation |
| 16.9 pts | Violence |
| 14.7 pts | Income |

**This is the substantive finding, not a technical caveat.** In Baltimore these are not five
separate causes competing for influence — they are five views of one concentrated pattern of
disadvantage. Communities with low income also have deteriorating housing, more violence,
more single-parent households and lower civic participation, to such a degree that the data
cannot tell them apart. Any claim that one of them is *the* driver would be reading precision
into numbers that do not contain it.

Two practical consequences. First, an intervention targeting one factor in isolation is
unlikely to move life expectancy, because the factor is not acting alone. Second, the
strongest evidence we have for any single lever remains income — not from these correlations,
but from the change-over-time test described in *How far do these findings hold up?*, which
is the only analysis here with real purchase on direction.

### Two cautions on reading these

**Civic participation appears surprisingly high** — voter turnout correlates +0.80 with life
expectancy, placing it among the top three measures. This is very unlikely to be causal. It
more plausibly marks communities with the social cohesion and institutional presence that
also sustain health.

**These describe places, not people.** A community with high poverty and low life expectancy
does not mean the poor individuals within it are the ones dying earlier — that inference is a
well-known statistical error. The correlations identify which *neighbourhoods* need
attention, not which *individuals*. This distinction matters when the findings are quoted.

## Disparities between Baltimore communities

### The gap is measured in decades, not percentages

Life expectancy in 2018 ranged from **63.2 years in Downtown/Seton Hill to 84.7 in
Cross-Country/Cheswolde** — a gap of 21.6 years between two communities in the same city.
Downtown/Seton Hill has a small residential population against a large daytime one and its
rates are unstable, so the more defensible figure excludes it: **18.3 years** between
Southwest Baltimore and Cross-Country/Cheswolde.

Comparing groups rather than extremes gives the most robust statement of all. Weighted by
population:

| | Mean life expectancy | Residents |
|---|---|---|
| The 10 worst-off communities | **67.1 years** | 90,118 |
| The 10 best-off communities | **80.3 years** | 109,438 |
| **Difference** | **13.2 years** | |

**31 of 55 communities fall below the citywide 72.7 years, covering 295,368 residents** —
more than half the city lives in a community with below-average life expectancy.

### Disadvantage concentrates in the same places

Across 112 indicators, we counted how often each community lands in the city's worst tenth.
If disadvantage were spread randomly, every community would score about 10%.

| Community | Share of indicators in the worst 10% |
|---|---|
| Cherry Hill | **31.2%** |
| Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park | **31.2%** |
| Greater Rosemont | 30.4% |
| Edmondson Village | 30.4% |
| Southwest Baltimore | 27.7% |
| Greenmount East | 25.0% |
| *city median* | *9.8%* |
| *Inner Harbor/Federal Hill* | *0.9%* |

The worst-off communities are in the bottom tenth on roughly **a third of everything
measured** — three times what chance would produce — while Inner Harbor/Federal Hill appears
there on 1 indicator in 112. Disadvantage in Baltimore is not distributed; it is stacked.

### Every disparity we tested has widened

Comparing the 90th percentile community to the 10th percentile, in the earliest and latest
year each indicator allows:

| Measure | Earliest | Latest | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adults without a HS diploma | 3.28× (2011) | **6.15× (2023)** | widened |
| Family poverty | 6.26× (2011) | **8.14× (2023)** | widened |
| Median household income | 2.72× (2010) | 3.26× (2023) | widened |
| Households with no vehicle | 4.30× (2011) | 4.48× (2023) | widened |
| Life expectancy | 1.15× (2011) | 1.17× (2018) | *see below* |

**Not one narrowed.** The educational gap nearly doubled: in 2011 the worst-off tenth of
communities had roughly three times the rate of adults without a diploma; by 2023 it was six
times.

Life expectancy is the one to read carefully. The ratio moved little (1.15× to 1.17×), and
the raw best-to-worst spread is unusable as a trend because the 2011 figure is inflated by
the Glen-Fallstaff error described later. Excluding that community, the spread runs 24.0
years (2011), 19.4 (2012), 21.6 (2018) — noisy, with no clean direction. **Life expectancy
disparity did not measurably narrow, but we cannot show it widened either.** The confident
widening claims above rest on the socioeconomic measures, which have cleaner series.

### Disparity tracks the city's racial geography

Baltimore's communities are segregated enough to compare directly: 29 are at least 70% Black,
14 are 30% Black or less.

| | ≥70% Black | ≤30% Black | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life expectancy (2018) | 70.3 yrs | 78.0 yrs | **−7.7 yrs** |
| Median household income | $48,052 | $102,262 | **−$54,210** |
| Family poverty | 21.1% | 8.1% | +13.0 pts |
| Households with no vehicle | 34.9% | 13.7% | +21.2 pts |
| Vacant/abandoned properties | 9.5% | 0.8% | **12× higher** |
| Overdose calls per 1,000 | 12.4 | 5.2 | +7.2 |

Residents of majority-Black communities live on average **7.7 fewer years** and their
communities hold less than half the median income. These are differences between *places*,
not between individuals, and they reflect the accumulated effect of how the city was built
and invested in — but the pattern is unambiguous and should be stated plainly rather than
left implicit.

### The widest gaps of all

Ranked by the ratio between the 90th and 10th percentile community (2023):

| Ratio | Indicator |
|---|---|
| **60.9×** | Vacant and abandoned residential properties |
| 33.0× | White (non-Hispanic) residents |
| 18.8× | Narcotics calls per 1,000 residents |
| 17.0× | Homestead tax credits per 1,000 units |
| 12.3× | Dirty streets and alleys reports per 1,000 |
| 12.1× | Overdose calls per 1,000 residents |

Property abandonment is the single most unequally distributed condition in Baltimore — some

### How many people this affects

|---|---|---|
| 271,837 | 46.6% | live in a community below the citywide median income |
| 191,170 | 32.8% | live where family poverty exceeds 20% |

## How strong is the evidence for navigation and advocacy services?

Barrier counts — how many households lack a car or an internet connection — show who *might*
struggle. They do not show that anyone actually missed care. Two indicators in this data do,
because they record whether people reached services that already existed:

- **Early prenatal care** — the share of mothers receiving first-trimester care
- **Child lead screening** — the number of children aged 0–6 tested

Of these, early prenatal care is the single strongest piece of evidence available.

### The finding that justifies the work

**In the worst-served communities, most mothers do not reach prenatal care in the first
trimester.**

| | 2018 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest community | 47.7% | **42.9%** |
| City median | 63.6% | 62.5% |
| Highest community | 87.1% | 93.5% |
| Spread across communities | 39.4 pts | **50.6 pts** |
| Communities below 60% | 18 of 55 | **21 of 55** |

In 2019, **21 of 55 communities** had fewer than six in ten mothers receiving early prenatal
care, and in the worst, **57% of mothers did not**. Prenatal care is routine, covered, and
universally recommended. A gap of fifty percentage points between neighbourhoods a few miles
apart is not explained by clinical need or by preference — it is a gap in reaching care. That
is precisely what navigation and advocacy services exist to close.

This is the strongest indicator to lead with, because it measures the *failure* the service
addresses rather than a condition that might cause one.

### An honest complication

The intuitive argument — no car or no internet means missed appointments — **does not survive
testing in this data.**

Each barrier correlates with missed prenatal care on its own, some strongly. But once
community income is held constant, those relationships largely disappear:

| Barrier | Correlation alone | After adjusting for income |
|---|---|---|
| No internet at home | −0.66 | −0.16 |
| No high school diploma | −0.70 | −0.36 |
| No vehicle | −0.48 | **+0.29** |
| Uses public transport to work | −0.51 | +0.14 |

*2019 figures. Negative means more barrier, less early care.*

Income by itself correlates **+0.74** with early prenatal care — stronger than any barrier
measure. And the no-vehicle relationship *reverses* after adjustment: among communities of
similar income, those with fewer cars do slightly **better**, which likely reflects that
car-free neighbourhoods in Baltimore are dense, central and close to clinics.

Two readings of this are possible, and the data cannot separate them:

1. **The barriers are incidental.** What actually keeps people from care is poverty, and lack
   of internet or a car is just a symptom of it.
2. **The barriers are the mechanism.** Poverty causes missed care *through* things like being
   unconnected and unable to travel. Adjusting for income then removes the very pathway of
   interest, and understates the barriers rather than debunking them.

The second is at least as plausible as the first, but proving it needs individual-level data
this analysis does not have.

### What this means for the case you make

**Lead with the utilisation gap, not the barrier counts.** The fifty-point spread in prenatal
care is direct, hard to dispute, and measures the problem itself. Barrier counts are best used
to describe *who* is affected and to size the operational task — Sandtown-Winchester's 58.5%
of households without a vehicle is a real constraint on how you deliver a service, whatever
its statistical relationship to outcomes.

**Expect the affordability question.** Because income dominates every access measure, a
reviewer may reasonably ask whether navigation help addresses the real obstacle or works
around it. The strongest answer is that navigation services routinely include benefits
enrolment, coverage applications and financial counselling — they act on cost, not only on
information. That case should be made explicitly rather than assumed.

**Do not overstate the transport argument in Baltimore specifically.** The vehicle finding
reverses direction under adjustment. Transport support may still be necessary for individual
clients, but community-level car ownership is not good evidence for it here.

## How far do these findings hold up?

We stress-tested the main claims rather than reporting them at face value. Three survive, one
needs qualifying, and one does not hold at all.

### Need is genuinely clustered, not scattered — confirmed

Testing the need scores against the boundary map gives a **Moran's I of 0.413** against
−0.019 expected if need were randomly distributed, significant at **p = 0.001**. Communities
with high need sit next to other communities with high need, far more than chance allows.

This is the statistical justification for a **place-based** strategy. If need were scattered
across the city, concentrating services in a few neighbourhoods would be the wrong design;
it is not scattered, so concentration is defensible.

### Income drives outcomes — strengthened

A correlation between poverty and poor health across communities may reflect nothing but
permanent differences between places. A stronger test asks whether communities whose
conditions *changed* also saw their health change. Comparing 2012 with 2018 removes every
fixed characteristic of a community:

| Condition | Correlation across communities | Correlation of *changes* |
|---|---|---|
| **Median household income** | +0.78 | **+0.49** |
| Shootings per 1,000 | −0.77 | −0.29 |
| Households with no vehicle | −0.76 | −0.26 |
| Family poverty | −0.71 | −0.21 |
| Housing violations | −0.80 | −0.13 |
| Unemployment | −0.68 | −0.04 |
| Families receiving TANF | −0.79 | −0.04 |
| Vacant/abandoned properties | −0.68 | +0.19 |

**Income is the only condition whose movement tracks movement in life expectancy.** Where
incomes rose, life expectancy rose. The others largely mark stable differences between
communities rather than forces acting on them — housing violations correlate −0.80 across
communities but only −0.13 in change.

A second test points the same way: 2012 income predicts 2018 life expectancy (+0.80)
slightly *better* than 2018 income does (+0.78). Conditions preceding outcomes is weak
evidence of direction, but it is the right direction.

Sobering context: **35 of 55 communities lost life expectancy** between 2012 and 2018, an
average of half a year. Oldtown/Middle East lost 5.1 years while Canton gained 3.4.

### The recommended five — qualified

We re-scored every community under 2,000 random weightings of the four service dimensions.
All five recommended communities stay in the top ten in **46%** of them; at least four do in
**84%**.

| Community | Median rank | Best | Worst |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greenmount East | 2 | 1 | 11 |
| Southwest Baltimore | 4 | 1 | 19 |
| Clifton-Berea | 6 | 3 | 15 |
| Upton/Druid Heights | 8 | 1 | 18 |
| Midway/Coldstream | 7 | 1 | **32** |
| *Sandtown-Winchester (not recommended)* | **2** | 1 | **8** |

Two honest qualifications follow. **Midway/Coldstream is the least stable** of the five,
falling as low as 32nd under some weightings — it is in the list largely on its eight-of-eight
persistence record. And **Sandtown-Winchester is more robust than three communities we did
recommend**, never ranking worse than 8th. It was excluded only on the persistence test, which
rests on the life-expectancy series alone.

Our judgment is to keep the five and treat **Sandtown-Winchester as a sixth candidate**,
flagging that the two criteria genuinely disagree about it. A reviewer who weights current
need above historical persistence should include it.

### What did not hold

The intuitive barrier argument for navigation services — no car or no internet causes missed
care — **does not survive adjustment for income**, as set out above. We report it as a
limitation rather than suppressing it.

## Limitations and data-quality concerns

### Errors found in the source data

**One implausible value distorts a headline finding.** Glen-Fallstaff's 2011 life expectancy
is recorded as **95.6 years** — the highest figure anywhere in fourteen years of data, and
not credible for a US neighbourhood. Every other year for that community sits between 77 and
82. Taken at face value it makes Glen-Fallstaff the steepest health decline in Baltimore, an
18.8-year fall that is entirely an artefact of a bad starting point. Our automated outlier
check missed it because in 2011 it *was* the city maximum, so it never looked extreme
relative to that year. We now treat 2012 as that community's baseline. **The implication is
broader: at least one more error like this may remain undetected.**

**The prenatal-care series has an unexplained break in the middle.** Citywide median
first-trimester care sits at 57–63% in 2010–2012, drops abruptly to 47–50% across 2013–2016,
then returns to 63% in 2017. The number of communities below 60% goes 18 → 43 → 48 → 19 over
the same span. Neighbourhood conditions do not swing that far in a single year and back
again; this has the signature of a definitional or collection change, not a health event.
**It means the middle years of this series should not be compared with either end**, and it
is the reason we make no claim about whether access is improving or worsening over time.
Comparing 2010 with 2019 alone, the *spread* between best and worst widened from 33.9 to 50.6
points — but that is driven by the best-served community pulling away (74% to 94%) rather
than the worst falling, and it deserves confirmation from a cleaner source before being
relied on.

**Four indicators are split across two spellings.** Measures such as "Percent of Population
18-24 Years old" and "...18-24 years old" differ only in capitalisation, which silently
separates the 2010 value from the 2015–2023 series. Tableau happens to merge them because it
ignores case; most other tools would not. These have been reconciled.

**Five percentage values fall outside 0–100**, and 1,366 values sit beyond three times the
interquartile range. Most of the latter are real — Downtown/Seton Hill genuinely is extreme
on many measures — but they were flagged rather than removed, and each should be checked
before it anchors a claim.

### Structural properties that constrain interpretation

**43% of the data is missing.** The workbook uses `--` for suppressed or uncollected values.
These were converted to blanks, never to zero — reading a suppressed rate as zero would turn
a data gap into an apparently healthy community. Reassuringly, the missingness is *not*
concentrated in poor neighbourhoods: it ranges only from 32.3% to 33.4% across all 55
communities, so incomplete data does not systematically erase the places we most care about.

**36 of 223 indicators are entirely empty** across all fourteen years and were dropped.

**Many indicators measure the same thing.** 41 pairs correlate above 0.9, and one pair is
perfectly redundant (`Businesses with Under 50 Employees` and `Total Number of Businesses`,
correlation 1.000). Fourteen distinct underlying constructs are each represented by multiple
indicators. This matters directly for our own scoring: **three of the nine components of the
Service Need Score fall within a single construct**, so poverty carries a third of the weight
rather than a ninth. The rankings are unlikely to change much, but the score is more
poverty-weighted than its description implies.

**Over half of indicators are heavily skewed** — 54 of 122 have a skew above 1 — so averages
mislead and map shading should use median or quantile breaks.

**Small communities produce unstable rates.** Downtown/Seton Hill has a small residential
population against a large daytime one; it is the most frequently flagged outlier in the
dataset and its rates should not be read like those of a residential neighbourhood.

**Seven communities were redrawn in 2020.** Boundaries changed alongside names — for example
Greenmount East became Oliver/Johnston Square — so before-and-after comparisons for those
seven mix real change with boundary change.

**Population is only counted in census years.** There is no 2023 population figure; the reach
estimates in this report use 2020 counts and predate any post-pandemic shift.

**Equal weighting is a judgment, not a finding.** The four service dimensions are weighted
equally because we had no principled basis for ranking them. A different weighting would
produce a different order. These scores are a screening tool for deciding where to look
first, not a measurement of how much need exists.

**Our need scores are relative, not absolute.** A percentile rank compares each community to
the other 54 in the same year. If conditions improved everywhere equally, no score would
move. These rankings cannot show progress over time, and should never be compared across
years as though they could.

### What is missing entirely

**Health insurance coverage.** The column exists in the workbook and is empty for all
fourteen years. This is the most serious gap — uninsurance is arguably *the* central
navigation-need variable, and this analysis is entirely silent on it.

**Disability.** No measure of any kind, in any year. A core social determinant and a direct
driver of advocacy need.

**English proficiency and language spoken at home.** We used percentage of Hispanic residents
as a proxy, which captures neither non-Hispanic limited-English households nor bilingual
residents who need no interpretation. Given that health-literacy need concentrates in
communities that are 31–45% Hispanic, this proxy is carrying more weight than it should.

**Any health outcome after 2019.** Life expectancy and mortality stop in 2018, birth outcomes
in 2019. We have no post-pandemic health data at all, and the pandemic plausibly changed both
the level and the geography of need.

**Healthcare utilisation.** No emergency-department visits, hospitalisations, avoidable
admissions, readmissions or missed appointments — the measures that would most directly
demonstrate navigation failure.

**Healthcare supply.** No clinic or provider locations, so we cannot distinguish a community
that lacks services from one that has them but cannot reach them. These call for very
different programmes.

**Mental and behavioural health.** Nothing beyond overdose and narcotics service calls, which
are policing data as much as health data.

## What we would want before committing a programme

Roughly in order of value:

| Data | Source | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Insurance coverage and Medicaid churn** | ACS table S2701; Maryland Health Connection | Fills the single largest gap; tells you whether the obstacle is coverage or navigation |
| **Current health outcomes** | CDC PLACES (census-tract estimates) | Replaces the five-year-old outcome data and tests whether the 2018 geography still holds |
| **ED visits and avoidable admissions** | Maryland HSCRC | The most direct available evidence of navigation failure — people using emergency care for treatable conditions |
| **Disability prevalence** | ACS table S1810 | Currently a complete blind spot |
| **Limited English proficiency** | ACS tables S1601 / B16004 | Replaces the Hispanic-share proxy with the actual measure |
| **Clinic and provider locations** | HRSA; Maryland Board of Physicians | Separates "no service nearby" from "service nearby but unreachable" |
| **No-show and missed-appointment rates** | Partner clinics, by agreement | Would settle the question this data could not |

Two of these would change the recommendation rather than merely refine it. **Insurance
coverage** could reveal that the binding constraint is affordability, in which case
enrolment assistance should lead and navigation follow. **Provider locations** could show
that a high-need community is already well served, moving it down the list.

Beyond quantitative data, a programmatic recommendation should not rest on neighbourhood
statistics alone. Everything here describes places rather than people — it identifies where
to look, not what residents actually experience. Listening sessions in the five recommended
communities, and conversations with clinics already serving them, would test whether the
barriers we inferred from census data are the ones people actually encounter. That is the
gap most worth closing before money is committed.
