# Income and the Prenatal Care Gap in Baltimore Communities

**Source:** BNIA Vital Signs, 2010–2023 · 55 Community Statistical Areas
**Prenatal care:** 2019 (the latest year collected) · **Need tiers:** 2023
**Companion to:** *Where Baltimore Communities Most Need Navigation, Advocacy, Literacy and
Caregiver Support* (`Service_Need_Findings.md`)
**Analysis:** `Income_Prenatal_Care_Deep_Dive.ipynb` · **Tableau file:** `tableau/Prenatal_Care_Deep_Dive.csv`

---

## The short answer

**Early prenatal care is where the gap between Baltimore communities shows up most directly,
and income is what organises it.** In 2019 the share of mothers who got care in the first
trimester ranged from **42.9% to 93.5%**, a spread of **50.6 points**. In the worst-served
community, **57% of mothers did not** get early care.

Income tracks this gap more closely than any access barrier does. Lacking a car, internet or
a high school diploma each goes with less early care on its own, but **once income is held
constant, every one of those links weakens or reverses**. We tested this in every comparable
year, not just 2019.

The communities with the worst prenatal care are mostly the ones already marked as high
need: **8 of the worst 10 are in the Highest or High tier**, against about half of all
communities. But **the two worst are not**, and they are also the two whose prenatal care
falls furthest below what their income would predict. An income-based or need-score-based
targeting list would miss them.

## The gap

| | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest community | 50.9% | 47.7% | **42.9%** |
| City median | 63.2% | 63.6% | 62.5% |
| Highest community | 82.9% | 87.1% | 93.5% |
| Spread across communities | 32.0 pts | 39.4 pts | **50.6 pts** |
| Communities below 60% | 19 of 55 | 18 of 55 | **21 of 55** |

In 2019, **21 of 55 communities** had fewer than six in ten mothers receiving first-trimester
care. The worst was **Orangeville/East Highlandtown (42.9%)** and the best was
**Greater Roland Park/Poplar Hill (93.5%)**. These figures match the existing findings.

The spread widened from 2017 to 2019, but mostly because the best-served community pulled
ahead (82.9% to 93.5%). The median barely moved. See *Can the years be compared?* before
reading any trend into this.

Single-year rates based on births in small communities are noisy. Ranking by the

### Overlap with the need tiers

| | In Highest/High tier | Share | Compared with chance |
|---|---|---|---|
| All 55 communities | 28 | 51% | — |
| Worst 10 for prenatal care | 8 | **80%** | 1.6× |
| Worst 15 for prenatal care | 12 | **80%** | 1.6× |

Poor prenatal care clusters in the high-need tiers at about **1.6 times the rate you would
expect by chance**. Across all 55 communities, need score and prenatal shortfall have a rank
correlation of **0.62**. That is strong, but well short of the same list.

**Three of the worst 15 are not high-need:** Orangeville/East Highlandtown (Moderate),
Cross-Country/Cheswolde (Lower) and Forest Park/Walbrook (Moderate). Going the other way,
**Oldtown/Middle East** is in the Highest tier but ranks in the better half for prenatal
care (65.8%).

*Caution: the tiers are built from 2023 conditions and prenatal care is from 2019. This
compares today's need with a slightly older outcome. It is not a same-year relationship.*

### How this relates to the recommended five

The five communities recommended in the service-need findings rank **12th to 26th** of 55
for prenatal care: Midway/Coldstream 12th, Southwest Baltimore 13th, Clifton-Berea 18th,
Upton/Druid Heights 21st, Greenmount East 26th. All five are below the city rate, so the
recommendation holds. But **a programme centred on prenatal care would begin somewhere
else**, most likely in the south and southwest (Westport, Brooklyn/Curtis Bay, Morrell Park)
and in the two outliers discussed below.

## Where the gap persists

Persistence counts how often each community falls in the city's ten worst, using only the
**six years that can be compared**: 2010–2012 and 2017–2019. This is the same test the
service-need findings use for life expectancy.

| Community | Worst-10 years (of 6) | 2019 | Need tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Westport/Mount Winans/Lakeland** | **6 of 6** | 50.8% | High |
| Orangeville/East Highlandtown | 4 of 6 | 42.9% | Moderate |
| Southwest Baltimore | 4 of 6 | 55.5% | Highest |
| Brooklyn/Curtis Bay/Hawkins Point | 3 of 6 | 51.5% | Highest |
| Glen-Fallstaff | 3 of 6 | 52.1% | High |
| Midway/Coldstream | 3 of 6 | 55.4% | Highest |

**Westport/Mount Winans/Lakeland is the one community in the worst ten every comparable
year.** It was also in the worst ten in 3 of the 4 excluded years, so its standing does not
depend on which years are left out. Southwest Baltimore shows the same pattern (3 of 4).

**The prenatal worst-ten changes much more from year to year than life expectancy's.**
32 of 55 communities appear in it at least once, and only one appears every time. For life
expectancy, seven communities are in the worst ten in all eight years. Part of this is
statistical noise from small numbers of births. **Two to four appearances out of six signals
a real, recurring problem. One appearance does not.**

## Income, not access barriers

### Income tracks early prenatal care in every year

| Year | 2010 | 2011 | 2012 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correlation with median household income | +0.62 | +0.67 | +0.69 | +0.74 | +0.79 | +0.74 |

Higher-income communities have more mothers reaching early care, and the relationship is
strong throughout. It is just as strong in the 2013–2016 years left out elsewhere
(+0.71 to +0.84). Income alone accounts for **about 55%** of the variation in early care
between communities in 2019.

### The barriers mostly stand in for income

| Barrier (2019) | Correlation alone | After adjusting for income |
|---|---|---|
| No internet at home | −0.66 | −0.16 |
| No high school diploma | −0.70 | −0.36 |
| No vehicle | −0.48 | **+0.29** |
| Uses public transport to work | −0.51 | **+0.14** |

*Negative means more of the barrier goes with less early care.*

Each barrier is itself closely tied to income (correlations of 0.73–0.82), so on its own
each looks like a cause. Once income is held constant:

- **No internet** and **no diploma** keep only a weak negative link.
- **No vehicle** and **public transport** reverse. Among communities of similar income, those
  with fewer cars do slightly *better*, most likely because car-free neighbourhoods in
  Baltimore are dense, central and close to clinics.

This is not a one-year result. In **all 18 barrier-and-year combinations** across the
comparable years, the link weakens or reverses once income is held constant. The vehicle
reversal appears in **all five** years it can be tested.

These figures match the existing findings to two decimal places.

*Method: partial correlation. Both prenatal care and the barrier are adjusted for median
household income, then correlated with each other. Repeating the test with income on a log
scale changes no conclusion. The only sign change is public transport in 2017, where the
figure is essentially zero either way (+0.01 vs −0.07).*

### What this does and does not mean

The same caution applies here as in the service-need findings. Two readings are possible:

1. **The barriers are incidental.** Poverty keeps people from care, and lacking a car or
   internet is just a symptom of it.
2. **The barriers are the mechanism.** Poverty causes missed care *through* things like
   being unconnected. Adjusting for income then removes the very pathway of interest.

Community-level data cannot separate these. What it does show is that **income is the
single best guide to where the gap is**. Any case for navigation services should be ready
for the affordability question, and should say plainly that navigation includes benefits
enrolment and coverage help, which act on cost directly.

### Where income does *not* explain the gap

Some communities miss early care far more than their income would predict:

| Community | Early care 2019 | Expected from income | Shortfall | Median income | Need tier | Hispanic residents (2023) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Orangeville/East Highlandtown** | 42.9% | 66.9% | **−24.0** | $60,457 | Moderate | 44% |
| **Cross-Country/Cheswolde** | 47.8% | 66.4% | **−18.6** | $58,844 | Lower | 6% |
| Madison/East End | 44.7% | 59.5% | −14.8 | $37,328 | High | 6% |
| Patterson Park North & East | 58.2% | 71.0% | −12.8 | $73,313 | Lower | 11% |
| Brooklyn/Curtis Bay/Hawkins Point | 51.5% | 60.1% | −8.6 | $39,162 | Highest | 31% |

**The two worst communities in the city have middling incomes and are not flagged as high
need.** Both a need-score approach and an income approach would under-target them. Their
shortfall points to something this data does not measure: language access, local provider
supply, or community-specific patterns of care. Orangeville/East Highlandtown's large
Hispanic population is a lead worth following, not an explanation. **These two warrant
direct inquiry before any placement decision is made about them.**

## Can the years be compared?

**Only partly.** The prenatal care series has a break in the middle.

| | 2010 | 2011 | 2012 | 2013 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| City median | 57.2 | 58.9 | 63.2 | *47.3* | *46.6* | *50.0* | *48.9* | 63.2 | 63.6 | 62.5 |
| Below 60% | 32 | 31 | 18 | *43* | *48* | *47* | *44* | 19 | 18 | 21 |

In 2013–2016 the median drops by more than ten points and then returns in a single year.
We treat this as a change in how the data was collected or defined, not a real health event:

- The four years form a separate group, **7.2 points below** every other year. No other gap
  between yearly medians exceeds 3.6 points.
- The citywide figure published alongside shows the same drop.
- **Communities kept their order through the drop and recovery.** Rankings across the break
  match from one year to the next as closely as in ordinary years. The whole city moved
  together, which is how a measurement change looks, not a neighbourhood-level health shock.

**What this means in practice:**

- **Comparable years:** 2010–2012 and 2017–2019. Persistence uses only these.
- **Do not compare levels** from 2013–2016 with any other year, or make any claim about
  whether access improved or worsened over the decade.
- **Rankings and correlations within a single year** are much less affected, so they are
  reported for 2013–2016 but labelled.
- 2010–2011 sit a few points below 2012 and 2017–2019. That is plausibly real, but it
  inflates their "below 60%" counts (32 and 31, against about 19 in later years).

Prenatal care was **not collected after 2019**, so there is no way to test whether the gap
has changed since.

## Child lead screening: available, not yet analysed

The workbook holds three lead-related fields:

| Field | Years | Usable? |
|---|---|---|
| Number of children aged 0–6 tested | 2010–2019 | Yes, with gaps: 47–52 of 55 communities after 2012 |
| Percent of children aged 0–6 with elevated levels | 2010–2019 | Yes, same gaps |
| Lead violations per 1,000 residential units | — | **Empty in every year** |

The screening measure is a **count**, not a rate, so it cannot be compared across
communities without a child-population denominator. The percent-elevated measure records
exposure, not access to care. Either needs that preparation before it can support a
finding like the one above.

## Using this in Tableau

`tableau/Prenatal_Care_Deep_Dive.csv` has one row per community and joins to every sheet
of the main workbook on **CSA**.

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `CSA` | Community Statistical Area (2010 boundaries), the join key |
| `prenatal_care_pct` | % of births with first-trimester care, 2019 |
| `need_tier` | Tier from `Service_Need_Profile` (2023) |
| `persistence_count` | Years in the worst 10 for prenatal care, out of 6 comparable years |
| `income` | Median household income, 2019 (same year as prenatal care) |
| `prenatal_rank` | 1 = worst, 55 = best |

**Watch the name clash:** `Service_Need_Profile` also has a `Persistence_Count`, but that one
counts **life expectancy** years out of 8. Rename one of them if both are in the same view.
